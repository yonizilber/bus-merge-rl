Bay or lane

Pick the conditions: how many people ride on the bus and in each car, how many passengers get on or off at the stop, how many doors are used, and how dense the traffic is. The tool tells you which design moves more people to the end of the road faster, from [[runs]] simulated runs of a bus stopping in a bay or in the lane.

Good to know: the experiment ran on a road with one lane and no overtaking. On a street with another lane, cars pass a bus standing in the lane, so the in-lane stop costs less. Which way the tool may mislead

Three of these limitations favour the bay and two favour the lane. We have not quantified them.

  • One lane, no overtaking (favours the bay): behind a bus in the lane the queue reaches 22 cars in the long stops of the simulation, while in the field (San Francisco) 1 to 3 were observed on average, because drivers overtake.
  • No long waits for a gap (favours the bay): in the simulation a merge takes at most [[mt_max]] s, and only [[mt_n30]] of [[mt_n]] bay runs passed 30. In the field, waits of 30 to 50 s were measured.
  • The bay's cost to the bus (probably favours the lane): [[bus_cost_range]] s in the simulation, about 2 to 6 measured in the field. See the comparison with field data.
  • A slow entry (probably favours the bay): the simulation lets about a third fewer cars per hour onto the road than the standard saturation flow.
  • The same stop time in every run (favours the lane): in the field it varies a lot, and that variation lengthens queues.

When the tool says "the lane is better", trust it more than when it says "the bay is better". The full comparison with field data is on the results page of the documentation.

The stop time and the bay's penalty

The stop time is chosen directly, not from the number of passengers. We did not derive it from passenger counts, because the measurements we found come from other systems (for example about 3.9 s per passenger in San Francisco before all-door boarding) and do not represent an Israeli city bus, with app validation and boarding at every door, which stops much more briefly. We found no Israeli measurement, so there is no "right" value here. Choose the time that fits the stop you have in mind, from 0 s: separately for the lane and the bay, equal in both, or equal plus a penalty for the bay.

Each design has accuracy penalties that make up the bay's penalty when you choose "equal plus the bay's penalty": the distance along the street from the stopping point, and the gap between the side of the bus and the kerb. An extra for entering the bay is added to them.

How the tool works

For every traffic rate and every combination of a stop time in the lane and in the bay we stored, over seeds in which both designs see exactly the same cars, the difference in the bus's trip time (a) and in all the cars' trip times (b), with their variances. The difference in person-seconds is bus passengers × a + people per car × b, so the browser can compute it for any occupancy without a new run. The stop times of the two designs are the base time and each design's accuracy penalties. The extra time to enter the bay is added to a. The confidence interval is a normal approximation of the mean. Between the experiment's points the tool interpolates linearly, and it never goes beyond the range that was tested (traffic 0.1–0.5, stop time 0–45 s).
